# Walkthrough: what the agent sees and what it does

Generated by `make_walkthrough.py` from the prompt templates in
`agent.py`, the baseline policy in `blind_par.py`, and saved episodes in
`runs/`. Nothing here is retyped, so it cannot drift from what ran.

## 1. The world

Places: table, sink, basin, counter, shelf. 
The two fixed objects never move (bowl on the counter, book on the shelf). 
The `cup` relocates every 8 ticks among table, sink, basin, 
and the task is to put the `cup` on the `shelf`.

The agent cannot see the room. `observe(place)` is the only action that
reveals anything, and it costs `L` ticks of world time. During those
ticks the `cup` keeps moving, so the answer is already out of date
when it arrives. Every other action costs 1 tick.

## 2. The prompt

This is the exact system prompt, at `L = 5`. The only thing that differs
between conditions is the number on the `observe` line; the stated and
applied values are set independently, so this number is not always the
one the world charges.

```
You are an agent acting in a room. You cannot see the room. The only way to learn anything about it is to use the observe action.

Places in the room: table, sink, basin, counter, shelf

Objects in the room: book, bowl, cup

The room runs on a tick clock. Your actions cost time:

  observe(<place>)        tells you which objects are at that place RIGHT NOW.
                          Costs 5 tick(s).
  goto(<place>)           moves you there. Costs 1 tick.
                          It tells you NOTHING about what is there.
  pick(<object>)          picks it up, and only works if you are already at
                          the same place as it. Costs 1 tick.
  place(<object>,<place>) puts down what you are holding. Costs 1 tick.
  done()                  ends the episode.

Important: cup does not stay still. It moves to a new place every so often, and it keeps moving while you are observing. An observation tells you where things were at the moment you looked, and the clock has advanced by the time you can act on it. The other objects never move.

Your task: put cup on the shelf.

Reply with exactly one action per turn, on its own line, in the form

ACTION: observe(sink)

You may write one short line of reasoning before it. Do not write anything after the ACTION line.
```

Each turn the agent replies with one action and receives one line back.
The reply it sees is a rendered result, for example
`[tick 5] At sink: cup.` or `[tick 6] Nothing to pick up here.`

## 3. The baseline (par)

Par is not a solved optimum. It is the mean over all sweep orders of a
blind policy that knows the rules but not any position, the same
information the agent has. Two approaches matter:

- **commit-on-sight**: look at a place; if the cup is there, walk over
  and pick. Cheap in observations, but at high `L` the cup has moved by
  the time you arrive.
- **walk-then-look**: walk first, then look from where you already are,
  so the sighting and the pick are adjacent. One extra move per place,
  and no travel for the cup to escape during.

At `L = 0` staleness is impossible and commit-on-sight is best. At
`L = 5` it stops working and walk-then-look takes over. That switch is
the mechanism the whole study is about, visible in a policy with no
learning in it at all.

## 5. How to read the numbers

| quantity | means |
|---|---|
| observations | how many times the agent paid to look |
| par | what a blind policy averages on the same seeds |
| success | referee's verdict on true state, never the agent's claim |
| turns | actions taken, so that looking less by taking longer shows up |

The comparison that carries the argument is not any single number. It
is the same latency applied to a world that moves and a world that does
not: about 23 observations against about 2.
